Editorial
As I write this for the inaugural issue of the TERI Alumni Quarterly, I find myself
reflecting on the remarkable journey of a community that has steadily grown
since the TERI Alumni Association was established in 2007. Today, our alumni
are spread across 28 countries, contributing to governments, academia,
industry, research institutions, and civil society. While your paths may have
diverged, you remain connected by a shared belief that knowledge must extend
beyond ideas to create meaningful change in the world.
What has always distinguished TERI alumni is not simply the time spent at
the Institute, but a way of thinking — grounded in scientific rigour, guided by
relevance, and driven by a commitment to addressing real-world challenges.
These qualities are needed now more than ever, as India navigates a defining
period in its climate, energy, and sustainability journey. It is heartening to see so
many of you helping shape the policies, innovations, and institutions that will
influence our collective future.
I hope this publication becomes a space where ideas are exchanged,
achievements celebrated, collaborations forged, and friendships renewed. I
warmly invite you to make it your own by sharing your work, your experiences,
and your stories. I look forward to staying connected with each of you through
these pages.
With warmth,
Dr Vibha Dhawan
Director General
The Energy and Resources Institute

Energy Policy Imperatives
Rakesh Kacker
Senior Consultant, Habitat Emprise
India crossed 300 GW of non-fossil generating capacity in July. Fossil fuels, mainly coal, still account for over 60% of demand.
International concerns over climate change have
dominated discussions on India’s energy policy in recent
years. This has led to a relative neglect of two important
domestic concerns: first, the imperative of meeting India’s
energy needs to fuel a rapidly growing economy and rising
living standards; and second, the long-standing issue of
domestic environmental impacts arising from greater
energy use, which in the short term is largely based on
fossil fuels that are far from clean.
India has, to its credit, been a responsible member of the
international community. The Nationally Determined
Contribution revised in 2022 raised the target share of non-
fossil fuel sources in installed electricity generation
capacity from 40 to 50%, with a target date of 2030, a
milestone achieved a year ahead of schedule. The revised
target now stands at 60% by 2035. The energy intensity
commitment was similarly revised upward, from a 35%
reduction over 2005 levels to 45%, now further revised to
47% by 2035.
India has thus remained well on course to fulfil the
commitments made through its NDC to the UNFCCC. But
alongside this, the country must also have enough energy
to meet domestic requirements and fuel growth.
Notwithstanding the impressive expansion in renewable
energy, coal-based capacity has continued to grow in
absolute terms. Peak coal production is not expected before
2040, and the share of coal in the electricity mix, while
declining proportionally, remains dominant in volume.
Three things at once
For electricity policy, this points to three things that need
to happen at once. The speed of renewable energy
integration in the grid must be accelerated. Existing coal
resources must be used as effectively as possible. And
domestic environmental concerns — pollution in air, water,
and soil from coal-based generation — must receive far
greater attention than they have in recent years.
India crossed the important milestone of 300 GW of non-
fossil fuel-based power generation capacity in July. Yet
fossil fuels, mainly coal, still account for over 60% of
demand. With demand growing rapidly, coal will remain
indispensable unless renewable capacity grows faster than
demand. This makes the case for complementing new
renewable capacity with adequate storage ever more
compelling. Recent tenders combining renewable energy
with storage reflect this recognition. The pace of
installation has, however, also been constrained by the
need for increased localisation, which caused a marked dip
in rooftop solar installations this year.
Cleaning up coal is part of the same transition
For the next several years, the country will have no option
but to continue depending on coal as the dominant fuel
supply source. Cleaning up coal usage and minimising its
adverse impacts, both in mining and utilisation, is
therefore not a secondary concern — it is part of the same
transition. The Economic Survey for 2023–24 stated this
plainly: while phasing in renewables to the extent possible
is imperative, in the short to medium term the focus should
also be on actively adopting clean coal technologies.
A related concern is the location of new coal-based
plants. The recent trend of siting these at load centres
means coal must be transported over long distances at
considerable cost, with implications for railway
infrastructure and ultimately for consumer tariffs. As
renewable penetration grows and these plants operate at
lower utilisation, fixed costs will be distributed over a
diminishing generation base, pushing tariffs higher still.
Approvals for such projects should be reconsidered, using
the full range of powers available to the Government
of India.
Energy transition is a large and complex task. These
observations are focused on the electricity sector and touch
on only a few of the many dimensions involved.
Considerable progress has already been made, and that
should give us confidence to address what lies ahead.

R&D in Cleantech: Does it Align
with India’s Net-Zero Ambitions?
Amit Kumar
Former Senior Director, Social Transformation & Knowledge Management, TERI
Outside electric vehicles and green hydrogen, most private research still runs in conventional, non-cleantech areas.
Photograph: Sergej on Unsplash.
The latest Research and Development Statistics at a Glance
2025–26 report by the Department of Science and
Technology justifiably highlights the growth in India’s
Gross Expenditure on Research and Development (GERD)
over the past decade. That India’s GERD as a percentage of
GDP reached 0.84% in 2023–24, up from 0.64% in 2020–21, is
a milestone worth acknowledging. The Global Innovation
Index 2025 places India 38th, noting that it leads Central
and Southern Asia and remains the top-performing lower-
middle-income economy. While the progress is real, a
closer look at international peers tempers this satisfaction.
Among BRICS nations, China, Brazil, and Russia
recorded GERD figures of 2.69, 1.19, and 0.94% of GDP
respectively. In ASEAN, Malaysia spent 1.43%. The
global average stands at approximately 1.8%. A
further feature of India’s R&D landscape is the
continuing dominance of the government sector,
Central and State governments and public sector
enterprises combined, which accounts for 48.2% of
R&D expenditure. The private sector contributes

Cleantech’s share of the research rupee
Looking at this sectoral expenditure through the lens of
clean technology, the picture is not encouraging. Among
the 12 major Central Government scientific agencies, DRDO
accounts for the largest share of R&D expenditure at 32.7%,
followed by the Department of Space at 15.0%, the
Department of Atomic Energy at 14.4%, ICAR at 13.1%,
CSIR at 8.7%, DST at 5.0%, and MNRE at a mere 0.1%. On a
conservative reading, only around 14% of the country’s
research spend, through CSIR, DST, MNRE, and a
portion of DBT, can be considered directed towards
cleantech domains.
The private sector picture is not much different. In
industrial R&D, transportation commands the largest share
at 32.6%, followed by drugs and pharmaceuticals at 21.4%,
biotechnology at 8.6%, information technology at 7.9%,
and electricals and electronics at 5.9%. Outside of electric
vehicles and green hydrogen-based mobility research, the
bulk of private sector activity continues in conventional,
non-cleantech areas. Aggregate statistics, in this way,
obscure the ground realities of India’s domestic energy
transition and decarbonisation landscape.
Innovation is today frequently conflated with the startup
ecosystem. India is rightly proud of its growing startup
numbers and its unicorns. Yet a review of the Startup India
database reveals that enterprises engaged purely in the
cleantech space remain comparatively sparse. On the
human capital side, India has 354 full-time equivalent
researchers per million population, against 2,415 in China,
2,680 in Russia, 895 in Brazil, and 7,287 in Singapore, well
below the global average of 1,486. Women researchers
represent 29% of the total in India, compared to over 40%
in Brazil, Russia, and South Africa.
What has to change
The limited focus on deep technological research in
institutions of higher learning and in core industry
deserves serious reflection. To operationalise India’s net-
zero ambitions effectively, this trend must be turned
around. While the National Education Policy encourages
research-oriented advanced courses and projects, the
ecosystem must move beyond publication-heavy academic
outputs toward engineering R&D geared to direct
technology development. That requires not only closer
academia-industry collaboration but faculty equipped with
genuine sectoral understanding.
The National Manufacturing Mission, with its ecosystem
approach, can put the country in a strong position in the
cleantech space. India’s leadership in solar energy is
undeniably impressive and hard-earned. But the next step
is to become a cleantech hub serving the global
community. True manufacturing leadership rests on a
strong indigenous R&D base, and the vision of genuine
atmanirbharta cannot be realised without substantial
investment in R&D.
India must urgently develop an R&D and Innovation
Roadmap that aligns with the goals set out in its Long-Term
Low-Carbon and Emission Development Strategy. That
roadmap must carry well-defined milestones across short,
medium, and long-term horizons. Research and innovation
are precisely the areas that require an aggressive policy
push, backed by adequate resources and greater research
autonomy. Concurrently, the private sector must step up to
the considerable commercial opportunities the energy
transition presents. In a geopolitical environment marked
by unilateralism, achieving comprehensive self-reliance is
a vital necessity for realising the vision of a Viksit Bharat.

What the World’s Grey Economies
Can Teach a Young India
Arvindd Narayanan
Former Research Associate, TERI  ·  Founder-Secretary, Pratyusha Foundation
This piece is written in response to NITI Aayog’s report Reimagining Care: Strategies for
Empowering Caregivers in Viksit Bharat@2047, published in July 2026.
India likes to think of itself as demographically young, and
it still is. But the NITI Aayog report is a useful reminder
that the countries currently wrestling with ageing
populations were once exactly where India is now, young,
confident, and unprepared. Japan, Germany, South Korea,
and the Nordic nations did not start out with sophisticated
care systems either. They built them, often under pressure,
and sometimes only after the pressure had become a crisis.
India has something those countries did not when they
began: the benefit of hindsight.
Who pays for care, and how?
The report’s global chapter is essentially a tour of the
different ways a country can answer one central question:
who pays for care, and how? Germany, Japan, and South
Korea have gone the route of mandatory social insurance,
where citizens contribute regularly so that care is available
on the basis of need rather than income. It is a model that
guarantees dignity regardless of wealth, but it depends on a
large, formalised workforce paying in, something India,
with its vast informal economy, would need to adapt rather
than copy wholesale. The Nordic countries and the
Netherlands fund care through general taxation,
prioritising equity over individual contribution. The United
States leans on private insurance, offering choice but
leaving affordability as an open wound. Southern Europe
(Italy, Spain, Portugal) relies on a residual model in which
the state steps in only after family resources are exhausted.
That model covers barely 10% of the elderly population,
and it is the closest thing to a cautionary tale in the report:
relying almost entirely on family care, without institutional
backup, simply does not scale once demographics turn.
That is the part worth sitting with. India’s caregiving
system today looks a great deal like Italy’s or Portugal’s,
deeply reliant on family, thin on institutional support. The
difference is that Italy and Portugal are already living with
the consequences of that gap, while India still has a runway
to close it before the elderly population triples by 2050.
No single imported model will fit
Where India is quietly getting things right is in recognising
that no single imported model will fit. Japan and South
Korea offer perhaps the most instructive parallel, because
both are civilisationally attached to family-based care,
much like India, and yet neither treated that as a reason to
do nothing. Japan introduced long-term care insurance
while simultaneously reforming its visa system to bring in
trained caregivers from abroad, protecting the family
model while building a professional one alongside it. South
Korea did something similar, blending home-based,
community, and institutional care into one coherent
framework rather than treating them as competing options.
That is precisely the posture NITI Aayog’s own
recommendations are reaching for: not replacing the
Indian family, but professionalising the ecosystem
around it.
Germany offers a second lesson, focused on the informal
caregiver. German policy does not simply train formal
caregivers; it extends financial and legal support to
informal ones, essentially paying and protecting family
members who provide care at home. India’s report gestures
at this through proposals like caregiving leave and social
security extensions, but the point is worth stating plainly:
this is the single most transferable lesson in the whole
report. India’s caregiving backbone is unpaid women. A
model that formally values that labour, even partially,
would do more for equity than almost any other single
policy lever available.
China is moving in a different direction, leaning heavily
into artificial intelligence and robotics to offset a caregiver
shortfall for its 310-million-strong elderly population, and
setting the first international standard for elder-care robots
in the process. India should not pursue this as a
replacement for human caregivers, and the report is right
not to frame technology that way. But as a force multiplier,
it is hard to ignore. A digital caregiver registry, tele-
monitoring for rural elderly, AI-assisted training modules:
these are lower-cost, faster-to-deploy versions of what
China is doing at scale, and they fit India’s resource
constraints better than a wholesale robotics push would.
Care as public infrastructure
If there is a single thread connecting Japan’s insurance
reform, Germany’s support for family caregivers, South
Korea’s integrated model, and China’s technology bet, it is
this: every one of these countries stopped treating
caregiving as a private family matter and started treating it
as public infrastructure. That is the real shift that NITI
Aayog’s proposed National Caregiver Council represents.
This is not a copy of any one country’s system, but India
finally deciding to build its own before the demographic
wave arrives rather than after. The world’s older economies
spent decades, and considerable pain, arriving at that
recognition. India has the rarer opportunity of choosing it
on its own terms, ahead of time.

Adopting Circularity:
Meeting India’s Solar
Energy Feedstock Challenge
Jaideep N. Malaviya
Former Research Scientist, TERI  ·  Managing Director, Malaviya Solar Energy Consultancy, Pune
End-of-life crystalline silicon modules awaiting recovery. Photograph: Malaviya Solar Energy Consultancy.
India has committed to 500 GW of renewable energy
capacity by 2030, with solar photovoltaics (PV) accounting
for 300 GW of that target. The scale of this ambition is well
understood. What is less discussed is the waste stream it
will generate, and the resource opportunity embedded
within it.
When those panels reach end-of-life, they will produce
in excess of 20 million tonnes of material. Nearly all of
India’s PV market, 98%, relies on crystalline silicon (c-Si)
modules, which are composed largely of glass (70% by
weight), aluminium frame (17%), polymer-based
encapsulant (5.1%), silicon metal (3.56%), copper (1.14%),
and smaller quantities of silver, tin, and lead. Every one of
these materials is either classified as critical or sourced
through net imports. India currently has no domestic
surplus of any of them.

The recovery opportunity
This is where circularity becomes not an environmental
imperative alone but an economic and strategic one. At 98%
recovery, a technically achievable benchmark, end-of-life
panels from India’s 2030 solar capacity could yield
approximately 16.8 million tonnes of glass, 4.5 million
tonnes of aluminium, 855,000 tonnes of silicon metal,
514,000 tonnes of copper, and 12,700 tonnes of silver. Each
of these, barring the polymer layers, can re-enter supply
chains as primary feedstock, reducing import dependence
and the carbon cost of virgin mining.
Silver makes the economics particularly clear. At just
0.05% of a module by weight, the value of reclaimed silver
at projected recovery volumes is enough to underwrite the
capital cost of setting up a recycling facility. If panels are
replaced earlier than their typical 25-year lifespan, which
several studies suggest is likely given rapidly improving
cell efficiencies and the economics of repowering, material
volumes could rise by a further 30%, strengthening the
business case further.
Policy is in place; infrastructure is not
The regulatory framework is in place. India’s E-Waste Rules
2022 mandate Extended Producer Responsibility
certificates for PV manufacturers, establishing
accountability across the value chain. What remains to be
built is the infrastructure and the green chemistry
approaches that make recovery economically self-
sustaining at scale.
With solar capacity projected to cross 1,000 GW by 2070
as part of India’s net-zero commitments, circular economy
operations in the PV sector will grow into a significant
industry in their own right. India has both the policy
architecture and the scale to build a global model for near-
complete, sustainable PV circularity — demonstrating that
what looks like a future waste problem is, in fact, a critical
materials opportunity.

Selective Solar
Experiences: 1990–2026
Dr Suneel Deambi
Solar energy expert and consultant
My first engagement with solar photovoltaics came as a
research student working under a DAE-funded project on
gallium arsenide (GaAs) solar cells. Highly efficient,
radiation-resistant, and temperature-tolerant, GaAs cells
have yet to gain significant ground in terrestrial
applications. A subsequent stint at NPL gave me further
laboratory grounding before I joined TERI in 1990, where
outdoor work opened up in ways that a laboratory
environment simply could not.
The verified concern
I recall with fondness Dr Akanksha Chaurey leading the
Solar PV team and giving me the opportunity to present my
views on various project activities. One that remains
particularly fresh in my memory was an MNRE-funded
project on the performance evaluation of solar PV power
plants (2–10 kWp) in Uttar Pradesh. It involved a
preliminary study of seventeen sites across both plain and
hilly regions, followed by a detailed technical study of two
sites. The interim report drew appreciation from the
Ministry, but a leading solar module manufacturer of the
time raised serious concerns about our reported efficiency
values. TERI stood its ground and was asked to demonstrate
its field testing procedure at a 20 kWp solar power plant at
the Motilal Nehru School of Sports, in the presence of
Ministry officials and the manufacturer’s team. It showed
just how seriously independent third-party evaluation of
field-installed systems was taken then. Such rigour is less
common today, even as installation capacities have grown
beyond all recognition.
Exceptionally low power solar and its appeal
I left TERI in 1995 to join the Science and Technology
Ministry, before finding my way back to solar through a
new venture, a pilot-scale manufacturing initiative for
polycrystalline thin-film cadmium telluride (CdTe)
modules. Every piece of imported equipment that arrived
at the plant, set up within an EHTP area, was received with
genuine excitement by an enthusiastic team. I recall a solar
products exhibition the company organised in Chandigarh,
displaying items such as mosquito repellants, window
ventilators, car ventilators, and a cap with an FM radio. The
response was striking: visitors came in large numbers, not
merely to appreciate these exceptionally low-power
consumer products but to express readiness for on-the-spot
purchases. Products like these could also have replaced
expensive dry-cell batteries in many homes. That they have
yet to reach households in any significant numbers remains
a quiet missed opportunity for the sector.
The newer dimensions
Much has changed for the better in the solar PV space since
those early years. Several flagship schemes have driven vast
numbers of installations across diverse segments of the
energy economy. Storage, once considered a weak link for
solar, is now central to the mainstream — megawatt-scale
solar and wind plants with battery energy storage systems
are increasingly viewed as a market necessity for supplying
firm and dispatchable renewable power. Some regions now
have surplus solar grid power, sitting idle for want of
sufficient transmission infrastructure — something that
would have been unthinkable a decade ago. Floating solar
is making quiet waves, as is the study of new crop patterns
beneath solar panels under agrivoltaic schemes.
The unwavering demand for subsidy
One thing that has not changed across these decades is the
expectation of subsidised solar products. The claim of
economically needy consumers is understandable. But the
same expectation persists among those of sufficient means.
An acquaintance who purchased an unsubsidised 8 kWp
rooftop solar system for Rs 4,00,000 in 2021 told me the
system has performed well and is close to recovering its
entire investment, yet he continues to regret not having
received any subsidy at the time. The question is worth
asking: is continued subsidy truly necessary to achieve
decarbonisation at pace, or has it become an expectation
that long outlasted the economic rationale that originally
justified it?
Solar seeking visibility
Solar systems make no noise. They sit quietly on rooftops of
housing societies, largely invisible to the everyday eye.
There is a case for using solar more imaginatively — in
garden lighting, fountains, signage, and landscaping — on a
scale that increases its public visibility and reinforces its
presence in urban life. Design innovation is also gaining
ground, from changes to the basic geometry of panels to
closer integration of solar with rooftop vegetation to reduce
temperature-related losses. Invisible solar panels for
building-integrated photovoltaic (BIPV) applications are
attracting interest. And near-complete silver recovery from
recyclable panels is becoming a practical reality, with
further innovative processes in development.
As I look back, TERI is where it all began for me — and
from there, it has been an unbroken journey in solar, right
through to consulting work that continues to this day.

Mutability of
Climate Sustainability
Prof. Anandajit Goswami
Research Lead, Ashoka Centre for People-Centric Energy Transition, Ashoka University; Director, Manav Rachna
Centre for Peace and Sustainability; Founder, TOHRI Foundation
The reflections expressed here are entirely personal.
The last twenty-four years of my journey in policy research,
covering energy, climate change, sustainability science,
and action, have been a constant experience of mutability.
Within that space, I have always found myself exploring a
world where no single truth or untruth holds for long.
Every aspect of sustainability has felt like a quantum
reality, with as many possibilities for truth and falsity as
one cares to count. Each research question I began
exploring led to a moment of apparent clarity, which, at the
next turn, revealed itself to be provisional, perhaps even
illusory. Truth became mutable, shifting into multiple
parallel worlds through which I have moved as a wanderer,
confronting a deep existential uncertainty at every stage.
That uncertainty has left what I can only describe as a
void of quantum existence, no determinism, no settled
micro- or macro-truth. Any question of climate action,
adaptation, mitigation, energy transition, or sustainability
science is, at its heart, about this very phenomenon: the
epiphany of a moment of truth giving way to another
mutation, opening onto a brave new world of possibility.
The thesis and antithesis of this mutability can only be
partially filled by creativity and expression, forms that
attempt to hold the same moment of truth from a different
angle. Like a wanderer or a mendicant, I have been unable
to settle on any single hypothesis within sustainability
science, energy, or climate change. The unsettled quality of
this pursuit has instead given rise to multiple births,
rebirths of creative multiverses, propelled by the very
disturbance of not knowing. It is those mutable truths that I
have sought to capture through art and through action.
Characters, images, notes, and melodies have emerged
from, and sometimes been consumed by, this existential
dilemma about the multiple possibilities and outcomes of a
single research question or hypothesis in climate change,
energy, and sustainability. Each is, in some sense, a
byproduct of that uncertainty, and occasionally a scapegoat
for it.
Yet the biggest realisation has been this: even the
moment captured in the lens of one’s creative binoculars,
however vivid and true it seems, will be preserved only in
the fourth dimension of changing time and space. Over
time, the truth will change. And with it, my own mutable
understanding of sustainability science and its meaning
will change too.

A TERI Alumni publication in the Concerned Citizen Series launched at TERI’s annual Darbari Seth Lecture 2025
The launch of Your City, Your Impact at the Twenty-fourth Darbari Seth Memorial Lecture, New Delhi, 20 August 2025.
Your City, Your Impact makes the case for greener,
healthier and more resilient cities in India. As

About TERI Alumni
Established in 2007, the TERI Alumni Association (TAA) is
a dynamic network that brings together past and present
members of TERI to continue the journey of
sustainability beyond the institution. Our alumni remain
leaders, innovators, and change-makers across sectors,
and together they strengthen the legacy of TERI by
building knowledge, nurturing talent, and driving
solutions for the planet.

A B O U T
Why Stay Connected
TAA has been steadily expanding its reach through
reunions, book launches, and thought leadership sessions.
Reunion events have brought together generations of
alumni to reconnect and build collaborations. TAA’s
knowledge-building initiatives, including the Concerned
Citizen Series, have produced eight significant titles
authored by TERI alumni. The Association also conducts
engaging webinars and talks on pressing issues ranging
from climate change and energy transitions to gender
empowerment and sustainable urban systems.
Alumni are at the heart of TERI’s legacy as well as its
future. Guided by TERI’s mandate to promote sustainable
development, our alumni serve as force multipliers,
carrying forward its impact across diverse domains
and regions.
